Agora's answer:

> Mill treats trade as a social act, so in principle it falls within society's jurisdiction (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)). He then separates interferences with trade into kinds, depending mainly on whether the principle of individual liberty is really at stake.
> 
> 1. Interferences that do not truly involve liberty. Restrictions on trade or production for trade are restraints, and "all restraint, quâ restraint, is an evil." But they affect only the part of conduct that society is competent to restrain. They are wrong solely because they fail to produce the results intended, which is the ground of the doctrine of Free Trade (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)). Mill says that this doctrine rests on grounds different from the liberty principle. The same holds for most questions about the limits of free trade, such as public control to prevent fraud by adulteration, sanitary precautions, or protections for workers in dangerous occupations. Liberty enters only in that leaving people alone is, other things equal, better than controlling them. Mill says that controlling them for these ends is in principle undeniably legitimate (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)).
> 
> 2. Interferences that are essentially questions of liberty. These are cases where the aim is to make a commodity impossible or hard to obtain. Mill's examples are the Maine Law, the prohibition of opium imports into China, and restrictions on the sale of poisons. He says they are objectionable not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)).

The question is whether government should do something for people's benefit instead of leaving it to individuals or voluntary combination (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 16 (edition-specific)). Mill says objections to such interference may be of three kinds (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 17 (edition-specific)). The first is that individuals are likely to do the thing better, which condemns government interference with ordinary processes of industry (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 18 (edition-specific)). He says the purpose of the union between male and female is "not barely procreation, but the continuation of the species," so it ought to last after procreation "so long as is necessary to the nourishment and support of the young ones" (John Locke, Second Treatise of Government, Book II, Chapter 7, §79, paragraph 3 (edition-specific)). > Burke argues that the new French Constitution severs the link between the people and their representatives. He says that "there is little, or rather no, connection between the last representative and the first constituent." In his account, a member of the National Assembly "is not chosen by the people, nor accountable to them" (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 326 (edition-specific)). Three elections, with two sets of magistracy between him and the primary assembly, make him "an ambassador of a state, and not the representative of the people within a state." Burke adds that, unless the people break up and level this gradation, "they do not at all substantially elect to the Assembly" (same, paragraph 326). He also says that any corrective would only plunge the system back into the tumult of popular election, which the gradation was meant to avoid. He calls this a "perpetual dilemma" caused by the "vicious, weak, and contradictory principles" chosen (same, paragraph 326).
> 
> Burke links this to the structure of the Assembly. He describes it as in effect a general congress of ambassadors from independent republics, with no constitutional means of coherence or subordination beyond their acquiescence in its decisions (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 318 (edition-specific)). He contrasts this with Britain. There the representative, "separated from the other parts, can have no action and no existence," and the government is a trustee "for the whole, and not for the parts." In France, by contrast, "the elective Assembly is the sovereign, and the sole sovereign" (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 325 (edition-specific)).
