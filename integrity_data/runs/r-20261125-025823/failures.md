> Burke argues that the new French Constitution severs the link between the people and their representatives, and that the Assembly is made sovereign instead of being a trustee for the whole nation.
> 
> On the missing connection: he says there is "little, or rather no, connection between the last representative and the first constituent." A member of the National Assembly is "not chosen by the people, nor accountable to them" (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 326 (edition-specific)). He points to three elections, with two sets of magistrates between the member and the primary assembly. This makes the member "an ambassador of a state, and not the representative of the people within a state" (same passage). Burke says that no corrective could change this. Any attempt to reconnect the representative with the primary electors would plunge the state back into the "tumult and confusion of popular election." He concludes that unless the gradation is broken up, the people "do not at all substantially elect to the Assembly" (same passage).
> 
> On the structure: he says the scheme tends to split France into independent republics with no constitutional means of coherence or subordination. The only link would be acquiescence in the decisions of a congress of ambassadors, and he says this is what the National Assembly in reality is (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 318 (edition-specific)).
> 
> He contrasts this with Britain. There, a representative, separated from the other parts, "can have no action and no existence," and the government is the point of reference for the whole representation. That government is "a trustee for the whole, and not for the parts." In France, by contrast, "the elective Assembly is the sovereign, and the sole sovereign" (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 325 (edition-specific)). He therefore calls the French Constitution "the very reverse" of the British in principle (paragraph 326, cited above).
> 
> He also doubts that the people's will is served by numbers alone. He says that the will and the interest of the many "must very often differ," and that a government of obscure attorneys and curates would not be good for twenty-four millions even if chosen by many more (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 93 (edition-specific)).
