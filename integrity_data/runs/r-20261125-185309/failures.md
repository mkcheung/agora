>

He argues that this makes society dangerously "valetudinary" (sickly, prone to invalidism), comparing it to taking regular doses of poison and provocatives to our love of liberty (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 112 (edition-specific)). >

This "distemper of remedy, grown habitual" wears out, by vulgar and prostituted use, the spring of spirit that should be saved for great occasions. He points to Roman boys practicing themes of tyrannicide in a servile period (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 113 (edition-specific)). He says that in ordinary times it produces the worst effects even on the cause of liberty (same passage).
>

Finding their extreme principles unfit for cases that need only a qualified, civil and legal resistance, they employ no resistance at all, since for them it is "a war or a revolution, or it is nothing." They come to think lightly of all public principle. > 
> 4. It makes constant projectors of change bad citizens. Those who keep some change in Church or State always in view regard the actual state as of no estimation. They rejoice in bad public management as more propitious to revolution, and they judge people and principles only by whether they advance their design of change. They swing between the most stretched prerogative and the wildest democratic ideas without regard to cause, person or party (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 113 (edition-specific)).
