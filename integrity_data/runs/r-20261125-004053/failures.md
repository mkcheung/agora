> Burke gives several connected reasons for disliking the constant talk of resistance and revolution.
> 
> 1. It turns an emergency remedy into a daily habit. He says he never liked "this continual talk of resistance and revolution, or the practice of making the extreme medicine of the Constitution its daily bread." He argues that this makes the habit of society "dangerously valetudinary" (that is, sickly and prone to invalidism). He compares it to taking periodic doses of poison or provocatives to our love of liberty. He also fears that the anniversary sermons commemorating the Revolution will "cheat many out of the principles" and the benefits of the Revolution they celebrate (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 112 (edition-specific)).
> 
> 2. It damages moral sentiments for no political gain. Burke says revolutionary politics "temper and harden the breast" for desperate strokes that are sometimes used on extreme occasions. Those occasions may never arrive, so the mind takes on a "gratuitous taint" and morals suffer when no political purpose is served. He adds that such people are so taken up with the rights of man that they have forgotten his nature (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 114 (edition-specific)).
> 
> 3. It encourages contempt for ancient institutions and a spirit of change. He dislikes revolutions whose signals are so often given from pulpits. He also dislikes the contempt for ancient institutions, set against "a present sense of convenience" (Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, Volume III, REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE., paragraph 46 (edition-specific)).
