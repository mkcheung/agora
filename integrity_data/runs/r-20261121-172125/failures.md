> 
> 1. Interferences that do not truly involve liberty. Restrictions on trade or production for trade are restraints, and "all restraint, quâ restraint, is an evil." They are wrong solely because they fail to produce the results intended, which is the ground of the doctrine of Free Trade (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)). Mill says that this doctrine rests on grounds different from the liberty principle. The same holds for most questions about the limits of free trade, such as public control to prevent fraud by adulteration, sanitary precautions, or protections for workers in dangerous occupations. Liberty enters only in that leaving people alone is, other things equal, better than controlling them. Mill says that controlling them for these ends is in principle undeniably legitimate (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)).
> 
> 2. Interferences that are essentially questions of liberty. These are cases where the aim is to make a commodity impossible or hard to obtain. Mill's examples are the Maine Law, the prohibition of opium imports into China, and restrictions on the sale of poisons. The question is whether government should do something for people's benefit instead of leaving it to individuals or voluntary combination (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 16 (edition-specific)). Mill says objections to such interference may be of three kinds (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 17 (edition-specific)). The first is that individuals are likely to do the thing better, which condemns government interference with ordinary processes of industry (Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 5, paragraph 18 (edition-specific)). > 
> **Sin.** A sin is not only a transgression of a law but also any contempt of the legislator, because such contempt breaches all his laws at once. It can therefore lie in a deed, in words, in omitting what the law commands, or even in the intention or purpose to transgress (Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 27, paragraph 2 (edition-specific)). The sin begins with resolving to carry out an act that tends toward it (Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 27, paragraph 2 (edition-specific)).
>

Hobbes finds this distinction in the Greek words for sin and crime and in the Latin *peccatum* and *crimen*. The Latin *crimen* means only sins that can be made to appear before a judge (Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 27, A Crime What, paragraph 3 (edition-specific)).

When civil law ceases, crimes cease, since only the law of nature remains. Then there is no place for accusation, and each man is judged only by his own conscience. A wrong act is then a sin but not a crime (Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 27, Where No Civill Law Is, There Is No Crime, paragraph 4 (edition-specific)).
